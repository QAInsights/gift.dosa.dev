The discount is real. The receipt is real. The saving is imaginary. Gift-card deals fail in predictable ways, and every one of them starts with the deal choosing your spending instead of your spending choosing the deal.

## Trap one: the store you never visit

Twenty percent off means nothing without a trip you were already taking. A $100 card for $80 at a shop across town is $80 committed to future errands, each one costing time and fuel. Before any deal, ask whether you would shop there this month at full price. Yes means the discount is real. No means the deal is the product.

## Trap two: the stockpile

Buying six discounted cards "for later" feels thrifty and behaves like a leak. Balances get forgotten, bonus portions expire, habits change, and one lost card wipes out the savings on the rest. Buy for the next few months of genuine spending. The next promotion will come. They always do.

## Trap three: the membership math

"Members save 15% on gift cards" is only a deal if the membership is already worth it. A $60 membership to unlock $15 of card savings is a $45 loss wearing a discount costume. Price the membership on its own merits first, then treat any card discount as a small extra.

## Trap four: the bonus countdown

Bonus value with a 30-day fuse is not $10. It is a timed errand. If rescuing it requires a special trip, the fuel and time quietly eat the face value. Value every bonus at what you would realistically rescue, and read our [discount versus bonus comparison](/library/discounted-vs-bonus-cards) before a big promotion tempts you.

## Trap five: the quiet fees and borders

Activation fees on prepaid network cards, currency mismatches on cross-border deals, and online-only or in-store-only limits each shave value. A $100 card with a $6 activation fee bought at 10% off is really 4% off. Read the fee line before the discount line.

## The rule that beats every trap

Only buy discounted cards for spending you already planned, at stores you already visit, in amounts you will use within months. Every exception needs a written reason. Deals that pass this rule are wonderful. Most deals, held against it honestly, excuse themselves.
