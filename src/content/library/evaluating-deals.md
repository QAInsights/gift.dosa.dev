Gift-card deals come in two shapes that look alike and behave differently: a lower price for face value, and a bonus attached to a full-price purchase. Learning to separate them takes a minute and saves real money.

## Do the face-value math first

A $100 card for $90 is a 10% discount on face value. Write both numbers down: what you pay, and what you can freely spend. Every restriction below subtracts from the second number, never the first.

## Price bonus cards separately

"Buy a $100 card, get a $10 bonus card" is not 10% off. Ask four questions about the bonus: when does it expire, where can it be used, what can it buy, and can it combine with other offers? A short expiry or narrow acceptance quietly shrinks its worth. Value the bonus at what you would realistically rescue from it, then decide.

## Read the limits that matter

Check the country and currency, whether the card works online and in-store, purchase limits, and membership requirements. A deal that requires a membership you would not otherwise buy has a higher true price than the sticker shows.

## Judge the seller, not just the price

From the retailer directly, terms are usually clear. From a marketplace or a stranger, read the buyer protection terms before anything else: who verifies the balance, the window for reporting a drained card, and whether refunds come back as cash or credit. Vague protection is part of the price.

## The one rule that beats every tactic

Only buy a discounted card for spending you already planned. A 10% discount on a store you would not have visited is not a saving. It is a reason to spend $90.
